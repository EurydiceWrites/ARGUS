# External Taxonomies — Survey

**Purpose:** Short reference for how five major UAP research bodies classify cases. Built as preparation for Phase 3 (Argus taxonomy design). For each system, the entry covers who and when, the unit of classification, the actual categories, strengths, and weaknesses. A short synthesis at the end names the axes that recur across systems and the axes unique to one or two of them.

**Source discipline:** Hynek and Vallée are written from primary-source familiarity (their books are still in print and widely cited). AARO, GEIPAN, and SCU are written from current web sources retrieved 2026-05-12, cited inline. Where a claim is uncertain, it is marked as such rather than presented as fact.

**Authorized by:** H-005 (Eurydice chat, 2026-05-12).

## 1. J. Allen Hynek (1972)

**Who and when.** J. Allen Hynek was an astronomer who served as scientific consultant to the U.S. Air Force's Project Blue Book from 1948 to 1969. He began the work as a skeptic and ended it as a serious researcher of the phenomenon. His classification system was published in *The UFO Experience: A Scientific Inquiry* (Hynek 1972).

**Unit of classification.** The case — a single sighting report or incident.

**The categories.** Hynek split cases first by distance, then by what happened.

*Distant observations:*

- **Nocturnal Lights (NL)** — lights seen at night, no other detail
- **Daylight Discs (DD)** — objects observed during daylight
- **Radar-Visual (RV)** — sighting confirmed by both eyewitness and radar

*Close encounters (within roughly 500 feet of the witness):*

- **CE-1** — visual observation, no physical interaction
- **CE-2** — physical effects on the environment or witness (scorch marks, electrical interference, etc.)
- **CE-3** — observation of occupants or beings

Later researchers extended this to CE-4 (abduction) and CE-5 (human-initiated contact), but those are post-Hynek and contested.

**Strengths.** Simple. Intuitive. The de facto common vocabulary in the field — most researchers know what "CE-2" means without a glossary.

**Weaknesses.** Doesn't classify on craft behavior, sensor modality, or witness phenomenology — only on distance and interaction kind. Has no category for cases where no craft is seen but anomalous events occur.

## 2. Jacques Vallée (1990)

**Who and when.** Jacques Vallée is a French-born computer scientist and astronomer who worked with Hynek and pushed the field toward broader phenomenological scope. His expansion of Hynek's framework is described in *Confrontations: A Scientist's Search for Alien Contact* (Vallée 1990) — though the researcher should verify the specific publication for any final paper, since the typology appears across several of his works.

**Unit of classification.** The event — broader than Hynek's sighting; includes events where no craft is visible.

**The categories.** A 4 × 5 matrix. Four event types, five intensity levels each.

*Event types:*

- **AN — Anomaly:** strange events without a visible UFO (anomalous lights, poltergeist-like reports)
- **FB — Flyby:** UFO seen at distance, no maneuver
- **MA — Maneuver:** UFO performing non-ballistic flight (rapid acceleration, hovering, right-angle turns)
- **CE — Close Encounter:** witness in proximity to the object

*Intensity levels (1–5):*

1. Sighting only
2. Physical evidence (traces, instrumentation, photos)
3. Living entities observed
4. Reality-transformation effects on the witness (perception alteration, time distortion)
5. Injury or death

Notation: an event might be coded "MA-2" (maneuvering UFO with physical traces) or "CE-4" (close encounter with reality-transformation effects on the witness).

**Strengths.** Captures the human-experiential side Hynek omits (level 4). Includes events with no visible craft (AN). The intensity axis is independent of event type — the same level scale works across all four event types.

**Weaknesses.** "Reality transformation" is hard to operationalize empirically; it depends on witness testimony in ways the other systems don't. The AN category is genuinely difficult to distinguish from non-UAP paranormal claims.

## 3. GEIPAN (1977–present)

**Who and when.** GEIPAN (Groupe d'Études et d'Informations sur les Phénomènes Aérospatiaux Non identifiés) is a unit of CNES, the French national space agency. It has been the official French UAP investigation body since 1977 and is currently the only government-run UAP body with a public case archive.

**Unit of classification.** The case — a testimony or sighting report.

**The categories.** Four primary classes, with subdivisions added in 2008:

- **Class A** — phenomenon fully identified after investigation (confirmed balloon, aircraft, planet)
- **Class B** — phenomenon probably identified, some uncertainty remains
- **Class C** — insufficient data to classify (testimony too thin, no corroboration)
- **Class D** — unidentified despite high-quality data
  - **D1:** strange phenomena with moderate consistency (single witness, no recording)
  - **D2:** very strange phenomena with strong consistency (multiple independent witnesses, photo/video, or physical traces)

The classification rests on two underlying criteria: **strangeness** (how anomalous the reported event is) and **consistency** (quality and corroboration of the data).

**Methodology.** A seven-step pipeline: testimony intake, file creation, initial analysis, investigation, classification, anonymization, publication. Cases are anonymized and published openly.

**Strengths.** Explicitly accounts for data quality (the C class). The D1/D2 subdivision gives a clean way to surface "interesting" cases (D2) without claiming they're "true UFOs." Institutional, methodologically conservative, public.

**Weaknesses.** Doesn't classify the *kind* of phenomenon — only whether it's explained. A D2 case could be a craft sighting, a humanoid encounter, or an unexplained sensor return, and the GEIPAN code alone doesn't distinguish.

## 4. AARO (2022–present)

**Who and when.** The All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office is the U.S. Department of Defense's official UAP investigation body, established in 2022. It absorbed the prior UAP Task Force.

**Unit of classification.** The case — typically a military or government incident report.

**The categories.** AARO does not use a Hynek-style phenomenological taxonomy. Their classification is centered on **resolution status** — whether the case can be explained:

- **Resolved / identified** — the object turned out to be a balloon, drone, aircraft, satellite, sensor artifact, or other known system
- **Unresolved / anomalous** — insufficient data to identify, or behavior that doesn't match known systems

AARO publicly states that "a very small percentage" of cases display anomalous signatures. Their reports also use *descriptive* terms — "orbs," "transmedium" (objects moving between air, water, and space domains), "metallic spheres" — but these are observed-shape labels, not a formal classification system with defined axes.

**Strengths.** Backed by sensor data (radar, infrared, satellite) that other systems rarely have access to. Defines clear what-this-is-not categories (drone, balloon, etc.) that other systems handle implicitly. Reports are now publicly released.

**Weaknesses.** No formal taxonomic axes beyond "resolved vs. anomalous." Public reports are filtered for classification reasons — many cases remain redacted. Doesn't engage with the human-experience side (witness phenomenology, occupant encounters) at all.

**Methodological note on AARO (Argus stance).** AARO's institutional position has drawn public criticism regarding the completeness and good-faith of its disclosures. For Argus, this means a deliberate asymmetry in how AARO is used: AARO releases — videos, images, case descriptions — are treated as **primary-source data**, usable as raw material for independent analysis. AARO's own classifications, resolution claims, and explanatory framings are **not adopted as ground truth**. Where Argus's independent analysis of an AARO-released item disagrees with AARO's published classification, that disagreement is itself a recordable finding rather than an error to be reconciled toward AARO's position. This is the standard archivist's stance toward any institutional source whose framing is contested in the public record.

## 5. Scientific Coalition for UAP Studies (SCU, 2017–present)

**Who and when.** SCU is a U.S.-based nonprofit founded around 2017 by scientists and engineers as a peer-reviewed research body. Not a government agency. They publish in venues including the Journal of Scientific Exploration and on Zenodo (an open-access scientific archive).

**Unit of classification.** The event or activity pattern — sometimes the single case, sometimes a cluster.

**The categories.** SCU does not have a single named classification framework like Hynek's CE-1/2/3. Instead they use **a behavioral typology of UAP activities** — focused on what the phenomenon is *doing* rather than how the witness experiences it. Their studies are organized around nine activity types:

1. Interactive flight (responding to observers)
2. Radical flight (extreme acceleration, right-angle turns)
3. Electronic transmissions
4. Interference with military weapons systems
5. Intrusions at military installations
6. Loitering
7. Close approaches
8. Observed occupants
9. Encounters with occupants

**Strengths.** Behavior-centric — captures what the object does, which is the most physics-relevant axis if you're trying to model the phenomenon. Peer-reviewed. Publishes raw data and source documents.

**Weaknesses.** Not a single canonical framework — different SCU publications use different cuts of the data. Less suited to single-case classification (a given case might fall into several of the nine types, or none).

## Synthesis — where these systems agree and diverge

**Axes that recur across systems (likely Phase 3 reuse candidates):**

- **Distance / proximity** — Hynek (distant vs. close), Vallée (CE category)
- **Physical evidence / sensor confirmation** — Hynek (radar-visual), Vallée (level 2), GEIPAN (the strangeness × consistency grid), AARO (sensor data emphasis)
- **Occupant / entity observation** — Hynek (CE-3), Vallée (level 3), SCU (types 8 and 9)
- **Anomalous flight behavior** — Vallée (MA category), SCU (interactive and radical flight), AARO (transmedium)
- **Resolution / explanation status** — GEIPAN (A/B/C/D), AARO (resolved vs. anomalous)

**Axes unique to one or two systems:**

- **Reality-transformation / witness phenomenology** (Vallée level 4 only) — including it imports the contested abduction-research debate into the taxonomy
- **Data-quality grading** (GEIPAN A/B/C/D) — most systems handle this implicitly or not at all
- **Military-specific behaviors** (SCU types 4 and 5) — only relevant for military-skewed archives; Argus's DOD videos and AARO-style cases fit, but FBI files less so

**Methodological asymmetry — AARO.** AARO is treated differently in Argus than the other four systems. Hynek, Vallée, GEIPAN, and SCU are scholarly or research-body sources whose frameworks Argus may borrow from on the merits. AARO is treated as a primary-source provider of *data* (videos, images, case descriptions) but not as a source of classifications Argus inherits. See the methodological note in section 4 for the full position.

**Observations for Phase 3 (these are observations, not recommendations — the design choice is yours):**

The five systems fall into roughly three camps. **What-the-witness-saw** (Hynek, Vallée) focuses on the human experience side. **What-the-object-did** (SCU, parts of AARO) focuses on observable behavior. **How-well-we-can-explain-it** (GEIPAN, parts of AARO) focuses on data quality and resolution. Argus could choose one camp as the primary axis, combine, or invent something new — but it should choose deliberately.

The "case" unit is shared across all five, with Vallée extending slightly to "event." This is broadly consistent with Argus's already-settled decision to classify *incidents*, though incidents may be finer-grained than what these systems mean by "case."

Vallée's intensity-level axis (1–5) is the only one that runs across event types as an independent dimension. Whether Argus borrows it depends on whether the archive supports level-3-and-up classifications, which it likely won't for many clusters (FBI files probably have few CE-3+ incidents; DOD videos have none, since there's no witness phenomenology in a sensor reel).

GEIPAN's data-quality grading is genuinely useful for an archival project — it gives a way to surface "interesting" cases (D2) without claiming truth. Argus may want something similar, though for institutional declassified material the relevant quality axes are different (chain of custody, agency, redaction level, original-source vs. summary-document) rather than testimony corroboration.

## Open questions for Phase 3 (carry forward to the next handoff)

1. Does Argus want a **what-the-witness-saw** primary axis (Hynek / Vallée camp), a **what-the-object-did** axis (SCU camp), a **resolution-status** axis (GEIPAN / AARO camp), or a hybrid?
2. Does Argus want a separate **data-quality** axis like GEIPAN's A/B/C/D? Given that the archive is institutional declassified material rather than witness testimony, "data quality" may need to be redefined in archive-relevant terms.
3. Does Argus include Vallée's intensity-level dimension as an independent axis, or treat intensity as flat / implicit in other codes?
4. All five surveyed systems classify whole *cases*. Argus has already decided to classify *incidents* (a finer unit). Does that change which axes carry over?
